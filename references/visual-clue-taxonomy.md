# Visual Clue Taxonomy and Collection Guide

Use this reference to avoid “single-clue tunnel vision” in image and video investigations. The goal is systematic coverage, not a claim that every pixel can be interpreted.

## Observation before interpretation

Keep these fields separate:

- **Observation:** what is directly visible, e.g. “white oblong EU-style plate; first three characters may be NEL.”
- **Interpretation:** what it may mean, e.g. “possible Polish regional registration prefix.”
- **Verification:** the external test, e.g. “compare against official or reliable plate-code references.”

Never rewrite an uncertain observation into a certain interpretation.

## Clue families

### 1. Text and writing system

Inspect:

- complete and partial words;
- diacritics and letter shapes;
- language, script, transliteration, spelling conventions;
- phone-number and postal-code formats;
- domain suffixes and social handles;
- date, time, currency, measurement, and price formats;
- typography, road-sign fonts, official templates;
- handwritten versus printed text.

Record alternate character readings. Search rare substrings independently.

### 2. Civic, institutional, and symbolic clues

Inspect:

- flags and color order;
- coats of arms and municipal logos;
- police, transit, postal, utility, university, and tourism branding;
- heraldic shields, crowns, crosses, stars, animals, and mottos;
- plaque layout and sponsorship logos.

A generic symbol may be common. Exact heraldic arrangements can be highly specific.

### 3. Vehicles and registration systems

Inspect:

- plate size, color, side bands, fonts, separators, region prefixes;
- inspection/registration stickers;
- dominant vehicle models and fleet liveries;
- road side and steering position;
- taxi markings, emergency vehicles, buses, bicycles, scooters;
- parking orientation and curb behavior.

Use regional fragments only; minimize personal data.

### 4. Roads and mobility infrastructure

Inspect:

- center/edge lines and colors;
- curb paint and materials;
- paving stones and tactile surfaces;
- bollards, guardrails, barriers, signs, signal heads;
- tram/trolley wires, rails, bus shelters, bike lanes;
- road drainage and snow-management features;
- pedestrian crossing design.

### 5. Architecture and construction

Inspect:

- roof pitch and covering;
- dormers, chimneys, eaves, gutters;
- window proportions and opening style;
- balconies, railings, shutters, air conditioners;
- brick, stone, render, wood, concrete, and insulation systems;
- floor count, setbacks, plot width, party walls;
- renovation age and building typology.

Use façade sequences, not one generic building, for exact matching.

### 6. Street furniture and utilities

Inspect:

- lamps, benches, bins, planters, tree grates;
- hydrants, utility cabinets, meter boxes, poles;
- manhole covers and drainage grates;
- fencing, retaining walls, playground equipment;
- public Wi-Fi signs, parking meters, charging stations.

Municipal procurement can make these surprisingly location-specific.

### 7. Public art and monuments

Inspect:

- silhouette, pose, material, scale;
- exact facial carving and clothing;
- crown, shield, weapon, book, animal, or emblem;
- plaque shape and position;
- damage, patina, moss, knots, grain, welds, repairs;
- base, landscaping, nearby fixtures.

A shared historical figure does not imply the same statue. Exact physical details are decisive.

### 8. Commerce and institutions

Inspect:

- business fragments and categories;
- awning, menu, clinic, hotel, school, pharmacy, bank, or restaurant design;
- local chains versus international chains;
- opening-hours syntax;
- delivery vehicles and branded umbrellas;
- visible unit numbers.

Business addresses are useful anchors but must be placed geometrically in the scene.

### 9. Physical geography and ecology

Inspect:

- coast, lake, river, canal, mountain, hill, plain;
- shoreline treatment and water level;
- geology, soil, sand, rock, erosion;
- tree and plant species, pruning style, crops;
- leaf state, bloom, snow, drought, and seasonal maintenance.

Avoid overprecision from common species or weather alone.

### 10. Light, weather, and temporal clues

Inspect:

- shadow azimuth and length;
- sun elevation and side lighting;
- cloud cover, haze, rain, wet ground, snow;
- artificial-light schedule;
- clothing and seasonal vegetation;
- temporary construction, advertisements, or events.

Use these to constrain ranges, not to manufacture exact timestamps.

### 11. Media provenance and manipulation

Inspect:

- screenshot borders and UI;
- watermark and credit lines;
- recompression and resampling;
- inconsistent blur/noise/shadows;
- clone, cutout, or generative artifacts;
- thumbnail crops and social-platform transformations.

Trace the earliest known version and compare uncropped copies.

### 12. Negative and relational clues

Record:

- features that should exist but do not;
- impossible adjacency or orientation;
- road rules inconsistent with a candidate;
- missing water/mountain/building expected from maps;
- seasonal or construction mismatch;
- object-to-road, camera-to-object, and foreground-to-background order.

Negative evidence is often the fastest way to reject a seductive near-match.

## Clue strength traps

- **Readable is not necessarily unique.** “King Casimir” may occur in many cities.
- **Unique is not necessarily reliable.** A two-pixel plate prefix can be misread.
- **Multiple web pages are not necessarily independent.** They may copy one source.
- **Correct venue is not necessarily the requested street.** Verify viewpoint geometry.
- **A strong clue cannot erase a direct contradiction.** Reassess the hypothesis.
